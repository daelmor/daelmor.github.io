## Introduction

Many years ago, in the Hellenistic age and in the Roman empire, and long before that, in the person of the philosopher knowledge and virtue were inseparable. His philosophy demanded to be lived by him; he himself was its vehicle. Living in harmony with his philosophical convictions was part of his philosophy. Today humanity has forgotten this — and not only humanity as a whole: within philosophical circles two great dangers are also lived out. On the one hand, the philosopher imagines that his philosophical discourse can suffice on its own without being bound to a philosophical life. Plato himself already perceived that danger when he wrote, to justify his decision to go to Syracuse: "I feared that I might appear in my own eyes to be a man of mere words, incapable of resolutely undertaking an action." On the other hand there is another danger, just as fatal: to believe it possible to do without philosophical reflection. The philosophical way of life must justify itself in reasoned and motivated discourse. That discourse is inseparable from the way of life. But how is a philosophical life practised? In this compilation I attempt to approach an answer through the great work of philosophers such as Professor Jorge Ángel Livraga, Delia Steinberg Guzmán and Pierre Hadot, who have already spoken of this subject in various texts and whose material is digested and ordered, with references to the philosophers of antiquity who originated these practices. I shall therefore say something about what "practical philosophy" is, and then go on to describe certain exercises common to almost all the philosophical schools of antiquity. I hope this work will, first, help me establish disciplines that allow me to practise those exercises, and second, that the reader may benefit from them. "A true philosopher is not one who knows by heart the definitions of Kant, Plotinus, Saint Thomas Aquinas or anyone else. No! The true philosopher is neither Aristotelian nor Kantian. The true philosopher is the simple man, the man who can interpret nature, the man who, even if left without books, could go on reading, and even if left without nature around him, could go on dreaming." (Jorge Ángel Livraga)

## Why practical philosophy?

For a long time an idea of philosophy was present among men, but they did not understand it, or they considered it a contribution to erudition. If we take the ancient Greek philosophers — Epicurus, Zeno, Socrates and the rest — we discover that the principal object of their science was the destiny of man and the means of attaining it. They therefore remained far more faithful to the true Idea of the philosopher than has been the case in modern times, in which the philosopher is found only as an artist of reason. Formerly these two concepts (reason, and the practice of reason) were not separated as they are today. The Stoics say, for example, that philosophy is "exercise", an "art of living" (Seneca). "It has been necessary to clarify what we mean when we propose that someone drawn to philosophical work may find not only answers to the perennial questions, but also patterns of conduct, concrete practices of demonstrated effectiveness across time, models designed by sages expert in the human soul. And since this is practical knowledge, something that must be exercised continuously, we have defined it as an 'art of living', following the idea of Epicurus, one of the worst understood philosophers and yet one of demonstrable present relevance." (M.ª Dolores F.-Fígares)

It is more important to desire the good than to know the truth. Erasmus affirms on many occasions that only he is a philosopher who lives in a philosophical manner, as Socrates, Diogenes and Epictetus did, but likewise John the Baptist, Christ and the apostles. It is highly significant that today we are recognising this again — that we have rediscovered, through the texts and through our teachers, that philosophy is not only a discourse but a way of life. "Virtue is a practical knowledge, composed of five qualities arranged in a cross, like the four cardinal points, around a centre occupied by wisdom. They are courage, _andreia_; temperance or moderation, _sophrosyne_; justice or probity, _dikaiosyne_; piety or devotion, _hosiotes_; and finally wisdom, _sophia_. This set of virtues constitutes the compass of conduct. The failure to use this compass to orient our acts is what arouses and provokes anxiety, frustration, fear, anger, the sense of guilt and so on — that is, the bad conscience." (F. Schwarz, _Sócrates, el camino de la felicidad_)

## Exercises

In all the schools of philosophy the principal factor or cause of human suffering comes from the passions: disordered desires and exaggerated fears. Philosophy appears as a therapy for these passions — "to strive to strip oneself of one's passions" (Friedmann). Each philosophical school has a different method of therapy, but all seek in that therapy a profound transformation in the individual's way of seeing and of being, as Pierre Hadot says: "We shall have to distinguish between the Stoic and Epicurean methods. The characteristics between the different schools are worth observing. The Stoics cure what is harmful by means of its opposite; the Pythagoreans advocate keeping the passions at a distance; Socrates cures homeopathically, redirecting, for example, love of earthly beauties towards eternal beauty." (_Spiritual Exercises_, Hadot, p. 231) The exercises seek to carry out that transformation. "It is not strange that in these times obsessed with health, philosophy should become a therapy for driving off the ills of the soul, and the reading of the classics of philosophy a way of escaping depression, the fear of poverty or the bitterness of failure in love. Philosophy has been, since its Greek origins, a wisdom of life, a search for salvation in an inhospitable world. Plato, Epicurus, Seneca, Augustine and so many others offer us wise counsel for orienting ourselves in life and attaining calm, serenity and, perhaps, a little happiness." (Juan Carlos del Río)

There exists no systematised treatise of exercises as such. It is, however, more frequent in the writings of the Hellenistic and Roman periods. Epictetus, for example, refers to the philosophical _topoi_, which relate to the three faculties of the soul: the desiderative, the active and the intellectual. Epictetus writes:

> "That is not all as regards the difficulties and dangers proper to the exercises; a suitable objective must also be set out. And what would that objective be? To make free use of our desires and aversions. What is to be said about this? Desires must not be frustrated, nor must we fall into what we wished to avoid. Here, then, is the aim of the exercise... Since we are accustomed to putting our desires and aversions exclusively at the service of things that do not depend on us, we have to accustom ourselves to acting in the contrary way." (Epictetus, _Discourses_, III, 12, 1–7)

Epictetus recommends acting directly at the moment desires and aversions present themselves. He also recommends beginning with small things and increasing the difficulty gradually.

Musonius Rufus wrote a treatise "On Practice". After a general introduction on the necessity of practising philosophy, he recommends physical exercise (becoming accustomed to the rigours of the weather, of hunger, of thirst), beneficial also for the soul in endowing it with strength and temperance, in addition to those other exercises proper to it alone. Thanks to this double exercise one grows accustomed not to fear what most men consider misfortune: poverty, suffering, death. It was Philo of Alexandria, however, who set out two texts with lists of exercises. The two do not coincide exactly, though they do mention mostly the same exercises. These are _Legum Allegoriae_ (Allegory of the Laws) and _Quis rerum divinarum heres sit_ (Who Is the Heir of Divine Things). In both he compiles, with explanation, Platonic, Pythagorean and Stoic practices. It is to these practices that the following lines are devoted, together with others more particular to philosophers such as Socrates and Plato.

### Attention

Attention constitutes the fundamental spiritual attitude of the Stoic. It consists in a continual vigilance and presence of mind, in a self-awareness always alert, in a constant spiritual tension. Thanks to attention the philosopher knows fully how he acts at each instant. Thanks to attention we notice what depends and what does not depend on us. On this point, some counsel: "You must not depart from your principles when you sleep, nor on waking, nor when you eat, drink or converse with other men." Epictetus (IV, 12, 7). The idea of tension (_tonos_) appears especially in Epictetus (IV, 12, 1–21). This concept is fundamental in Stoicism. Attention may also be seen as vigilance, as concentration centred on the present moment: "In any situation, and constantly, it depends on you to rejoice piously in what happens at that particular moment, to conduct yourself evenly with all those around you, and to examine with a certain rigour the present representation, provided that your thought admits nothing inadmissible." (Marcus Aurelius, _Meditations_, VII, 54). Among the advantages of attention are these: it eases vigilance by concentrating it on a brief instant, always masterable, always bearable; it opens our consciousness to cosmic consciousness, obliging us to discover infinite value in each instant and to accept every moment of existence. To develop attention it is necessary to keep the philosophical principles we adopt always to hand (memorisation); imagination, affectivity and meditation must work together to allow us to set before our spiritual eyes the moment we are living. It is here that we shall lean on the other exercises in this list.

### Meditation and recollection of what is beneficial

The exercise of meditation makes it easier to be prepared for the moment when an unforeseen, perhaps dramatic, circumstance arises (Pierre Hadot). One must present oneself in advance with the problems proper to existence — poverty, suffering, death — and must look at them squarely, remembering that they are not evils, since they do not depend on us. In memory we shall recover those forceful maxims that will help us, when the moment comes, to accept the events that form part of the course of Nature.

Advice for carrying out this meditation:

- In the morning, examine beforehand the activities to be undertaken through the day, establishing the principles that will govern and inspire them. In the evening they will be analysed again to give an account of the failings or the progress made.
- Dreams should be submitted to examination.
- Meditation is a matter of mastering inner discourse in order to make it coherent.
- These reflections may be made in a dialogue with oneself, or with others, or by resorting to writing.
- They require training, so it is advisable to develop a habit in order to be able to do it daily.
- It is good also to recall and to write a small diary gathering the experiences and lessons lived each day, so as to be able to keep track of our progress.

This is to live as a philosopher, conscious of the meaning and of the price of one's existence. As Delia Steinberg Guzmán says: "He who knows how to go to the heart of all things will be able to go to his own heart."

### Reading

The reading of the sayings of poets and philosophers are the practices that will help our reflection or meditation to be nourished. Reading would also include the explanation of properly philosophical texts, of works written by the masters of the philosophical schools.

### Study and examination in depth

These involve putting into practice the teachings read and learned from instructors.

### Mastery of oneself

Possibly one of the most difficult, owing to our failure to understand the vital law concerning what depends and what does not depend on us. There are many writings on this that might help. From Plutarch: _On the Control of Anger_, _On Tranquillity of Soul_, _On Brotherly Love_, _On Love of Children_, _On Talkativeness_, _On Curiosity_, _On Love of Riches_, _On False Modesty_, _On Envy and Hatred_. Seneca, for his part, would also compose works of the same kind: _On Anger_, _On Benefits_, _On Tranquillity of Soul_, _On Idleness_.

### Fulfilment of duties

In the fulfilment of duties, it is recommended to begin exercising oneself with the simplest things, so as to establish the habit progressively and solidly.

### Indifference towards indifferent things

This expression corresponds to the exercises of Marcus Aurelius: "To live always in perfect happiness: the soul can find vigour within itself if it shows indifference in relation to indifferent things." It also echoes the definition of the purpose of human life coined by Ariston. Indifference is not to be understood as "absence of interest", but on the contrary as feeling an identical affection (without establishing any "difference") for each of the instants of existence.

### Learning to dialogue

The practice of exercises probably developed in traditions reaching back to time immemorial, but it is Socrates who will bring them to the surface of Western consciousness, since his figure was, and remains, a living call to the awakening of moral consciousness. (Pierre Hadot, _Spiritual Exercises_, p. 34) It is notable that such a call should have been made heard in the form of dialogue. In the "Socratic" dialogue the real question is not "what is being spoken of" but "he who speaks". When one observes Socrates closely and begins to converse with him, even if one has begun by speaking of some other subject, in the end one is carried by the thread of the discourse in many directions, until the moment when one is obliged to give an account of oneself — both of the way one lives at present and of the way one has conducted one's existence in the past. At that point Socrates will not let us go before all this has been submitted to the test of his authority.

Dialogue appears to us as an exercise inviting an examination of consciousness, a directing of attention upon oneself (Know thyself). The relation with oneself constitutes the foundation of any exercise. To know oneself is to recognise oneself as "not wise", that is, as _philo-sophos_.

The dialogue must then be held with an interlocutor. That interlocutor may be outside, or within. In any case, even when he is outside, the examination we make of ourselves is always inward: "Set yourself to meditate now and concentrate deeply; by whatever means, fold back upon yourself as you concentrate. If you stagnate at any moment, run quickly to some other part... Do not always centre your thought upon yourself; rather let your spirit begin to fly through the air like a bumblebee held to the ground by a thread." (Plato)

Antisthenes was once asked what profit he had drawn from philosophy, and he answered: "That of being able to converse with myself." Only he who is capable of a true encounter with the other is in a position to meet himself authentically — and the reverse is also true. Dialogue becomes truly dialogue only in the presence of another and of oneself. From this point of view every philosophical exercise is dialogical, in so far as it presupposes a genuine exercise of presence, both before oneself and before others. In the philosophical exercises it is necessary to oblige oneself to change point of view, attitude and conviction; and therefore to dialogue with oneself means, at the same time, to struggle with oneself.

## Conclusions

In antiquity philosophy made no distinction between reason and the practice of reason. Coherence between one's way of thinking and one's way of living was a requirement of the philosopher. That is something we as humanity must remember and take up again. As for the practice of philosophy, the schools of antiquity, however much they might differ doctrinally in philosophical discourse, all had in common the practice of virtue in order to bring about the cessation of man's suffering. Among these exercises there are certain ones common to all philosophical movements, which seek to put the teaching into practice: attention, reflection or meditation, reading, and dialogue, among others.
