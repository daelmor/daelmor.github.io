## Introduction

Many years ago, in the Hellenistic age and in the Roman empire, and long before that, in the person of the philosopher knowledge and virtue were inseparable. His philosophy demanded to be lived by him; he himself was its vehicle. Living in harmony with his philosophical convictions was part of his philosophy. Today humanity has forgotten this — and not only humanity as a whole: within philosophical circles two great dangers are also lived out. On the one hand, the philosopher imagines that his philosophical discourse can suffice on its own without being bound to a philosophical life. Plato himself already perceived that danger when he wrote, to justify his decision to go to Syracuse: "I feared that I might appear in my own eyes to be a man of mere words, incapable of resolutely undertaking an action." On the other hand there is another danger, just as fatal: to believe it possible to do without philosophical reflection. The philosophical way of life must justify itself in reasoned and motivated discourse. That discourse is inseparable from the way of life. But how is a philosophical life practised?

In this compilation I attempt to approach an answer through the great work of philosophers such as Professor Jorge Ángel Livraga, Delia Steinberg Guzmán and Pierre Hadot, who have already spoken of this subject in various texts and whose material is digested and ordered, with references to the philosophers of antiquity who originated these practices. We shall focus in particular on the exercise of the Socratic dialogue. "A true philosopher is not one who knows by heart the definitions of Kant, Plotinus, Saint Thomas Aquinas or anyone else. No! The true philosopher is neither Aristotelian nor Kantian. The true philosopher is the simple man, the man who can interpret nature, the man who, even if left without books, could go on reading, and even if left without nature around him, could go on dreaming." (Jorge Ángel Livraga)

## Why the Socratic dialogue as a philosophical exercise?

For a long time an idea of philosophy was present among men, but they did not understand it, or they considered it a contribution to erudition. If we take the ancient Greek philosophers — Epicurus, Zeno, Socrates and the rest — we discover that the principal object of their science was the destiny of man and the means of attaining it. They therefore remained far more faithful to the true Idea of the philosopher than has been the case in modern times, in which the philosopher is found only as an artist of reason. Formerly these two concepts (reason, and the practice of reason) were not separated as they are today. The Stoics say, for example, that philosophy is "exercise", an "art of living" (Seneca).

"It has been necessary to clarify what we mean when we propose that someone drawn to philosophical work may find not only answers to the perennial questions, but also patterns of conduct, concrete practices of demonstrated effectiveness across time, models designed by sages expert in the human soul. And since this is practical knowledge, something that must be exercised continuously, we have defined it as an 'art of living', following the idea of Epicurus, one of the worst understood philosophers and yet one of demonstrable present relevance." (M.ª Dolores F.-Fígares) It is highly significant that today we are recognising this again — that we have rediscovered, through the texts and through our teachers, that philosophy is not only a discourse but a way of life. The Socratic dialogue leads us to an inner examination, to reflect on our own incoherences and to try to live in such a way that we attain the greatest good. "Virtue is a practical knowledge, composed of five qualities arranged in a cross, like the four cardinal points, around a centre occupied by wisdom. They are courage, _andreia_; temperance or moderation, _sophrosyne_; justice or probity, _dikaiosyne_; piety or devotion, _hosiotes_; and finally wisdom, _sophia_. This set of virtues constitutes the compass of conduct. The failure to use this compass to orient our acts is what arouses and provokes anxiety, frustration, fear, anger, the sense of guilt and so on — that is, the bad conscience." (F. Schwarz, _Sócrates, el camino de la felicidad_)

## Maieutics

Socrates's method of teaching was called by his student Plato "maieutics" — that is, the art of giving birth, since in a certain way he acted as a midwife. He is like a guide who leads his students through the cave of false opinions to come out into the light of the real. His teaching brings the student face to face with himself, with his own limitations, so that he may break them and go beyond. With skilful questions he brings the interlocutor to such a degree of inner tension that, if he makes the "leap", he discovers or kindles within himself the flame of what is certain.

> "Because teaching is a priesthood, it requires the magnetic presence of the Master, who draws out the Inner Guide we all carry within. Although the fire sleeps in the wood, someone is needed to kindle it, and that is the Master." (José Carlos Fernández)

## The Socratic dialogue

As a tool for the maieutic method, Socrates used the spoken word. He taught that transmission must be oral, and that writings are nothing but a reminder, a synthesis of what has been said. In reading a written work many interpretations and ambiguities may arise about the sense the author meant to give it. There is besides, in the voice and in real (not virtual) presence, a magic we cannot ignore.

Professor Jorge Ángel Livraga, in his _Cuadernos de Filosofía Moral_, writes of the Socratic dialogue: "It hardly needs stressing to what extent the mystical-religious factor was implicit in all Socratic teaching. The dialogue did not have as its object the mere verbal agility of the contenders; it enclosed the profound significance of a play of ideas destined to clarify, to bring to light, to draw out the sleeping knowledge from the depths of the human soul. This dialogue need not always be between two people, nor need it take on the characteristics of a conversation; Socrates's idea was to turn each of his students into a good 'dialoguer' with himself. Here we find a junction with the Pythagorean doctrine cited earlier: the inner search is synonymous with the examination of conscience — man must learn to be a better judge of his own errors, discovering and correcting them before anyone else does."

Socrates developed dialogue as part of a method of teaching. He preferred to give birth to consciences rather than to add knowledge cumulatively. He inclined towards taking pains to find good questions. If the answer was mistaken, he would ask another question that allowed the interlocutor to think again and to arrive, little by little, at a correct answer — a sufficient truth, which could then be used to reach a greater one.

## Learning to dialogue

The practice of exercises probably developed in traditions reaching back to time immemorial, but it is Socrates who will bring them to the surface of Western consciousness, since his figure was, and remains, a living call to the awakening of moral consciousness. (Pierre Hadot, _Spiritual Exercises_, p. 34)

It is notable that such a call should have been made heard in the form of dialogue. In the "Socratic" dialogue the real question is not "what is being spoken of" but "he who speaks". When one observes Socrates closely and begins to converse with him, even if one has begun by speaking of some other subject, in the end one is carried by the thread of the discourse in many directions, until the moment when one is obliged to give an account of oneself — both of the way one lives at present and of the way one has conducted one's existence in the past. At that point Socrates will not let us go before all this has been submitted to the test of his authority. Dialogue appears to us as an exercise inviting an examination of consciousness, a directing of attention upon oneself (Know thyself). The relation with oneself constitutes the foundation of any exercise. To know oneself is to recognise oneself as "not wise", that is, as _philo-sophos_. The dialogue must then be held with an interlocutor. That interlocutor may be outside, or within. In any case, even when he is outside, the examination we make of ourselves is always inward: "Set yourself to meditate now and concentrate deeply; by whatever means, fold back upon yourself as you concentrate. If you stagnate at any moment, run quickly to some other part... Do not always centre your thought upon yourself; rather let your spirit begin to fly through the air like a bumblebee held to the ground by a thread." (Plato)

Antisthenes was once asked what profit he had drawn from philosophy, and he answered: "That of being able to converse with myself." Only he who is capable of a true encounter with the other is in a position to meet himself authentically — and the reverse is also true. Dialogue becomes truly dialogue only in the presence of another and of oneself. From this point of view every philosophical exercise is dialogical, in so far as it presupposes a genuine exercise of presence, both before oneself and before others.

In the philosophical exercises it is necessary to oblige oneself to change point of view, attitude and conviction; and therefore to dialogue with oneself means, at the same time, to struggle with oneself.

We can investigate and find splendid advice for dialogue: to raise one subject only; respect and appreciation for others; improving our handling of language and its level; avoiding rigid and dogmatic psychological positions; reviewing our beliefs and prejudices with a longing for objectivity; seeking the truth rather than setting out "our truth" at all costs. But there is something essential: to dialogue, one must know how to listen. Knowing how to listen is much more than being in front of someone who is talking. It is receiving the words of others with attention, receiving them as new, without "scanning" them according to our prejudices, and striving to understand them. It would be very interesting to set ourselves a small exercise, something practical: let us listen to others consciously, as though for the first time, even when it is someone very familiar whom we deal with daily; let us listen and give value to their words, from the first to the last.

## How to dialogue with oneself?

The exercise of reflection (we might also say: meditation) makes it easier to be prepared for the moment when an unforeseen, perhaps dramatic, circumstance arises (Pierre Hadot). One must present oneself in advance with the problems proper to existence — poverty, suffering, death — and must look at them squarely, remembering that they are not evils, since they do not depend on us. In memory we shall recover those forceful maxims that will help us, when the moment comes, to accept the events that form part of the course of Nature. Advice for carrying out this dialogue, meditation or reflection:

- In the morning, examine beforehand the activities to be undertaken through the day, establishing the principles that will govern and inspire them. In the evening they will be analysed again to give an account of the failings or the progress made.
- Dreams should be submitted to examination.
- Meditation is a matter of mastering inner discourse in order to make it coherent.
- These reflections may be held with oneself, also by resorting to writing.
- They require training, so it is advisable to develop a habit in order to be able to do it daily.
- It is good also to recall and to write a small diary gathering the experiences and lessons lived each day, so as to be able to keep track of our progress.

This is to live as a philosopher, conscious of the meaning and of the price of one's existence. As Delia Steinberg Guzmán says: "He who knows how to go to the heart of all things will be able to go to his own heart."

## What to dialogue about?

Below are some examples of subjects we can use to begin the inner dialogue.

**Study and examination in depth.** These involve putting into practice the teachings read and learned from instructors.

**Mastery of oneself.** Possibly one of the most difficult subjects, owing to our failure to understand the vital law concerning what depends and what does not depend on us. There are many writings on this that might help. From Plutarch: _On the Control of Anger_, _On Tranquillity of Soul_, _On Brotherly Love_, _On Love of Children_, _On Talkativeness_, _On Curiosity_, _On Love of Riches_, _On False Modesty_, _On Envy and Hatred_. Seneca, for his part, would also compose works of the same kind: _On Anger_, _On Benefits_, _On Tranquillity of Soul_, _On Idleness_.

**Fulfilment of duties.** In the fulfilment of duties, it is recommended to begin reflecting on the simplest things, so as to establish the habit progressively and solidly.

**Indifference towards indifferent things.** This expression corresponds to the exercises of Marcus Aurelius: "To live always in perfect happiness: the soul can find vigour within itself if it shows indifference in relation to indifferent things." It also echoes the definition of the purpose of human life coined by Ariston. Indifference is not to be understood as "absence of interest", but on the contrary as feeling an identical affection (without establishing any "difference") for each of the instants of existence.

**Pain.** Pain is a vehicle of consciousness. When we feel a pain (physical, energetic, emotional or mental) it is a call to attention, to reflection; it is a perfect opportunity to exercise the Socratic dialogue. We can begin with a question: what hurt me today? — which is another way of asking, what bad thing happened to me today? Why did I feel sad, overwhelmed, desperate, angry? After the answer we shall find another symptom, for which another "why?" must be sought, and so on until we reach as deep as we can in order to identify the real cause, or at least to come near it. If anything was decisively characteristic of the Socratic dialogues, it was the search for truth — moving away from opinions, finding the essence of things, clarifying what a word meant, the real motive of something, the search for the ideas that give rise to all the rest, clarifying the axioms. To make the inner examination we must move away from our own opinion and try to be objective with our questions and our answers.

## Conclusions

In antiquity philosophy made no distinction between reason and the practice of reason. Coherence between one's way of thinking and one's way of living was a requirement of the philosopher. That is something we as humanity must remember and take up again. Maieutics (the art of giving birth) is Socrates's method of teaching, which does not aim to accumulate knowledge but to draw out of the interlocutor the truth that is within him. The Socratic dialogue is the tool used in maieutics by Socrates, in which, by knowing how to listen and by asking the right questions, he brought the interlocutor to an examination of himself. Dialogue is a tool that can be used by the philosopher in an inner dialogue, where the highest conscious part converses with the other elements of our personality.
