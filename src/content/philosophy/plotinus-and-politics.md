## Introduction

Plotinus is a figure as considerable as he is mysterious. I shall not say much about his biography, but I will mention an interesting expedition in which his place was probably that of a philosopher of the kind who sometimes formed part of a Roman imperial retinue. This in turn suggests that he already had access to the influential circles he would later move in at Rome. The expedition failed and the emperor died. Plotinus escaped to Antioch in Syria and from there went to Rome. Neoplatonism is not usually associated with politics — rather with questions of the Soul and its movements, with unification, and so on. Yet Plotinus does make reference to political matters, though not always directly. He also had episodes in his life bound up with politics, including the attempt to found Platonopolis, the city of the philosophers. This chapter takes up the life of Plotinus in Rome and his approach to political questions.

## Plotinus in Rome

In Rome, Plotinus was received into the house of a certain Gemina. There he created an unofficial circle, or philosophical school. It is interesting that, of the fourteen regular and committed members of this circle mentioned by Porphyry, three were women (Gemina, her daughter, and Amphiclea), most came from the eastern part of the Empire, five were politicians (three of them senators), three were physicians and two men of letters. Of the three senators — Orontius, Sabinillus, Rogatianus — the last appears to have been a military prefect and proconsul of Asia, who caused a great impression by renouncing high office, while Sabinillus was consul with the emperor Gallienus.

Plotinus's philosophical circle, informal and open to visitors and to other less regular members, was organised along the characteristic lines of a philosophical school of antiquity: the texts of Plato were read and explained, publications were prepared, and Plotinus was assisted in this work by two close and devoted students, Amelius and Porphyry. Amelius, from Etruria, was Plotinus's student and assistant in Rome from 246 to 269, when he left to live at Apamea in Syria, where he went on promoting his master's works. Porphyry joined the circle much later, in 263.

To this philanthropic work Plotinus added the activity of unofficial arbiter and judge of disputes, apparently to the satisfaction of the judicial authorities. In Porphyry's _Life of Plotinus_ one sees a model of practical virtue, of benevolent and disinterested stewardship and of just arbitration, exercised alongside the contemplation of transcendent principles.

Something more ambitious seems to have been intended in the project with which Plotinus approached the emperor Gallienus, who, with his wife Salonina, held the philosopher in high esteem. Plotinus proposed to revive a "city of philosophers" that had long lain in ruins in Campania: if the emperor granted the surrounding lands, it would be settled (Plotinus would join it, with his companions), would operate according to the laws of Plato, and would be called "Platonopolis". Opposition at court, however, blocked the project. Some questions arise from this initiative, as mysterious as it is poorly documented. What was this city of philosophers, and where? A Pythagorean or neo-Pythagorean community of the kind that existed in Magna Graecia? Or perhaps Cicero's "Academy"? What sort of city did Plotinus wish to establish? A kind of monastic community of otherworldly philosophical ascetics? Or did Plotinus mean somehow to realise the archetype of Plato's Republic, as the reference to Plato's laws suggests?

These questions remain unanswered for as long as we lack clarity about Plotinus's theoretical position on the relation between philosophy and political life. For some reason the project was never carried out. Plotinus nevertheless remained active in his philosophical circle at Gemina's house, composing his treatises until, in 268, the circle began to disperse; illness led him to withdraw to the estate of Zethus, where he went on writing and where he died in 270.

## Politics

On politics there is much to say — both in the sense that not much was written about it, and in the sense that there are small nods towards it by way of other subjects, and some initiatives, such as Platonopolis. It is the task of philosophy to free the soul, to provide it the means of reaching a higher plane of existence where it will find the perfection of life it seeks. Since the perfection of life may be described as "divine", godlike, the purpose of philosophy can be defined as the divinisation of man, the "assimilation of man to God so far as is possible" — words from Plato's _Theaetetus_ that the Neoplatonists made their motto. In this light, an interest in political questions can apparently only be a distraction. The very purpose and spirit of Neoplatonic philosophy would therefore seem to exclude political thought.

The belief that the aim of Neoplatonic philosophy excludes in principle any interest in political philosophy is confirmed, it seems, by several facts, at least in the strict sense of how politics is perceived. In particular, we are told that Plotinus encouraged his friends and students to withdraw from political life:

> Another of his companions was Zethus, an Arab by race... a close friend of Plotinus, a man with political inclinations whom Plotinus would try to restrain from political life. (Porphyry, _Life of Plotinus_)

> Rogatianus, a senator, advanced so far in renouncing this life that he gave up all his property, dismissed all his servants and resigned his rank. When he was about to appear in public as praetor and the lictors were already there, he refused to appear or to have anything to do with the office. Plotinus looked on him with great favour and praised him highly, and frequently held him up as an example to all who practised philosophy.

Neither does Neoplatonism figure significantly in the standard histories of political philosophy. Neoplatonic philosophy appears to keep silent: the monarchical ideologies of the Hellenistic and Roman periods are followed by the theocratic and anti-theocratic programmes of the Christian theologians (Eusebius of Caesarea and Augustine) and by the monarchical ideas of some pagans of late antiquity — one of them Themistius, who does not belong to mainstream Neoplatonism; another Julian, an emperor and a philosopher. This silence of the Neoplatonic philosophers is really broken only by their immediate heirs in early times, such as the Islamic world, where, unexpectedly, Plato's political works and Platonic political philosophy take on considerable importance.

It would seem that the divinisation of man excludes political philosophy. Yet as the Neoplatonists understood it, far from excluding political life, it actually includes it. The philosophers of late antiquity who withdrew from political participation could call on illustrious predecessors. There is above all the example of Socrates, who explains his idiosyncratic conduct in the following way (in Plato's account) to the court that was about to condemn him: "It began in my early childhood — a sort of voice which comes to me... this is what prevents me from entering public life, and a very good thing too, in my opinion; because you may be quite sure, gentlemen, that if I had tried to go into politics long ago, I should have lost my life long ago, without doing any good either to you or to myself. No man on earth who conscientiously opposes you or any other organised democracy, and flatly prevents a great many wrongs and illegalities from taking place in the state to which he belongs, can possibly escape with his life. The true champion of justice, if he intends to survive even for a short time, must necessarily confine himself to private life and leave politics alone."

Even if he did not in fact seek political responsibilities, Socrates was nevertheless very active at the centre of Athenian life — indeed, for him it must have been rather difficult to avoid — working at his self-appointed mission to reform the morals of his city. And his withdrawal from politics did not prevent his execution in 399 BC.

> Those who have become philosophers and have tasted how sweet and blessed the possession of philosophy is, when they have also fully realised the madness of the majority, that practically nobody ever acts sanely in public affairs, that there is no ally with whom one might go to the aid of justice and live — then, like a man who has fallen among wild beasts, he is not willing to share in wickedness and is not strong enough to oppose the general savagery alone, since he would perish uselessly for himself and for others before he could benefit his country or his friends. Taking all this into account, he keeps quiet and minds his own business. Like a man who takes shelter behind a small wall from a storm of dust or wind-driven hail, and sees other men full of lawlessness, the philosopher is satisfied if he can somehow live his present life free of injustice and impious acts, and depart from it with a beautiful hope, blameless and content.

Another heir of the Socratic withdrawal is the Stoic sage, who retires to the inner citadel of his freedom of judgement, indifferent to and above the vicissitudes of politics — even if, like Marcus Aurelius, he is emperor.

Political philosophy will then have to do with the study of social structures, of the principles of human social organisation and of their realisation (in constitutional order, legislation, enforcement and jurisdiction) to the extent necessary to achieve, at least in part, the human good.

## Divinisation and politics

Plotinus begins his treatise by quoting the "assimilation to god" passage from Plato's _Theaetetus_, where this assimilation is bound up with the endemic presence of evils "in this place" and with the need to "escape" them. This assimilation, as "escape", does not mean going somewhere else, as Plotinus stresses to the same effect in another treatise. Rather, the "escape", the divinisation, means becoming virtuous, becoming "just and pious with wisdom" — again in the words of the _Theaetetus_. A problem arises at once, however. Plotinus, following Plato, has suggested that becoming godlike means becoming virtuous. Yet how could this be? The virtues involved are human. So how can the virtues make us godlike if the gods are above human virtue? Plotinus resolves this difficulty in Plato's text as follows. The virtues we must cultivate are, in the first place, the four cardinal virtues defined by Plato in the Republic: wisdom, courage, moderation and justice.

Plotinus calls these virtues "political". They have to do with the correct functioning of the various parts of the soul, and in particular with the governing function that reason must exercise in relation to the soul's irrational parts. These last are connected to the relation the soul has with the body. The "political" virtues thus have to do with the soul in its relation to the body, and require essentially the rule of reason in that relation, as against a life subject to the irrational, chaotic and unlimited impulses of material desire. This is why the gods are above such virtues: in their serene sovereignty they are unaffected by the psychic disorder and confusion that is human vice. Yet Plotinus insists that in cultivating the political virtues "we may be said to be divine: they constitute a first level on a scale of divinisation that goes beyond them to a higher moral level, that of the purifying virtues, which emancipate the soul from its preoccupation with the body and bring it nearer the perfection of divine life." Plotinus therefore points to the idea of a hierarchy of moral excellence, a scale of virtues, of which the first level — that of the "political" virtues — is presupposed by higher moral states, those brought by the "purifying" virtues that constitute a greater degree of approximation to divine life. From this we may conclude that the practice of the "political" virtues is a first step on the path of divinisation, whose more advanced stages are hardly accessible without the prior acquisition of these virtues.

## Conclusions

We may conclude that what makes the virtues of a "political" life is the degree to which it expresses the divine within the context of the relation between soul and body.

The two functions might further be compared with two movements that govern the allegory of the cave that Plato develops at the centre of the Republic: the ascending movement by which a prisoner is freed from the darkness of ignorance and opinion and brought out of the cave into the light of the knowledge of the Good; and second, the descending movement by which the freed prisoner, the philosopher, must return to the cave to put his newly acquired knowledge at the service of his fellow prisoners. This double movement expresses well the complexity of the relation between political philosophy and the aim of divinisation in Neoplatonism: if political philosophy has a function in relation to the first stage of divinisation, it must be surpassed, transcended, if the process of divinisation is to continue; yet it returns again in relation to the divinisation of political life, the transformation of the life of the soul in relation to the body into something better, something more divine.
