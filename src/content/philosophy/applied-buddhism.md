## Introduction

In his first sermon the Buddha taught the Four Noble Truths: the truth about suffering, the truth about the origin or cause of suffering, the truth about the cessation of suffering, and the truth about the path that leads to that cessation (the noble eightfold path). He presented them in two groups: the cause and result of Samsara, and the cause and result of nirvana. Samsara refers to a state of existence characterised by the predominance of suffering. Nirvana refers to the state of liberation from suffering, and also to the cessation of its causes. [7]

When we examine these truths, we see that the first is the result caused by the second. The third truth is the result reached by way of the fourth. So we see a correspondence and a flow between these truths, which give us not only an understanding of life, of universal karma and of transcendence into Nirvana, but also a formula to apply in our everyday life.

In this "applied Buddhism" I have wanted to bring into the present the knowledge of teachers from different fields — philosophy, psychology and medicine — in order to apply it within the formula of the four noble truths set out by the Buddha, so that as individuals, as members of a family, as teachers or as students, we can make use of these reflections to improve our inner life.

The term "applied" is normally used in science, but I use it deliberately as a way of saying that our understanding of reality can be used to help clarify and to seek a way of transforming a particular situation in everyday life. Beyond that, this article attempts to look at the painful situations of our lives in three groups: medicine, emotional events and financial circumstances. Each one in the light of the four noble truths.

My aim in this work is that we manage to recognise ourselves in the exercise of reflection, through the teachings of the Buddha.

## Before beginning

### Advice for finding the cause of suffering

> "It is essential to understand that what happens to us has a reason, and that destiny, life, the gods, or whatever one wishes to call the chain of causes and effects, is not a capricious accident." [9]

Some points we ought to bear in mind in our reflection in order to find the causes:

- Suffering has a cause. It does not exist simply because we are good or because we are bad.
- The cause is not to be found outside us. In our reflection we must look inward.
- We must do deep work to find whatever is closest to a primary cause within our own being. To manage it we must ask ourselves, "Why is this happening to me?" On finding that answer A, we must go on asking, "Why does A happen?", and we will arrive at answer B, and so on until we find the deepest cause that carries an action we can take in order to begin working on it.
- We are the owners of our destiny, and, as the Buddha said: "the circumstances we find ourselves in today are the result of what WE have thought."

## Medicine

### The existence of suffering

#### Focusing questions

What is the illness? What is the problem here? Where is the suffering?

#### Reflection

We begin from the notion that everything in this world is suffering, that suffering exists and is therefore part of a "normal" process. Illness, then, is a manifestation of that universal dynamic. To quote Dethlefsen, in _The Healing Power of Illness_:

> "...illness is a state which indicates that the individual, in his consciousness, has ceased to be in order or in harmony..."

> "...illness has but one purpose: to help us remedy our «faults» and make us whole."

### The cause of suffering

#### Focusing questions

What caused it? Can it be cured?

#### Reflection

The search for the cause of an illness should lead us, after ever deeper repetitions, into psychic ground, where we ought to find the concept or idea that started it all. "The body is never ill or well, since only the information of the mind is manifested in it. The body does nothing of itself... what occurs in the body of a living being is the expression of a piece of information, or the concretion of the corresponding image (image in Greek is _eidon_, and refers also to the concept of «idea»)." [2]

### The cessation of suffering

#### Focusing questions

What is health? What is it that one is trying to achieve?

#### Reflection

We must reflect on the goal we wish to reach. Surely a sick person wishes to attain health in order to end their suffering. "For the understanding of illness and of healing it is important to understand what healing really means. If we lose sight of the fact that healing always means an approach toward the health encoded in unity, we will look for the goal of healing within polarity, and failure is certain." Healing, then, health, is a search for harmony and balance — realised on the etheric-physical-pranic plane, but achieved through harmony and balance on the psychic plane (emotional and mental).

The way to free oneself from suffering, once its cause is known, is non-attachment. If we understand the element causing the psychic imbalance that led us into a state of physical disharmony, we must detach ourselves from the desire for that element.

### The way of the eightfold path

#### Focusing questions

Which words, thoughts, attitudes and actions that led me to this state must I change in order to achieve balance and harmony in my mind and body?

#### Reflection

"The fourth noble truth provides a therapeutic scheme, the Eightfold Path, for relieving suffering and reaching an ideal state of health. The Eightfold Path comprises: right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration, right view and right thought. These eight practices tend to encourage and perfect the three foundations of Buddhist training and doctrine, namely: 1) ethical conduct, 2) mental discipline and 3) wisdom." [3]

## The fear of loneliness, and other emotional pains

### The existence of suffering

#### Focusing questions

What is it that I feel? How can I describe my feelings at this moment?

#### Reflection

"Depression means oppression, contraction or dejection. It describes a disturbance of mood, transitory or permanent, characterised by feelings of dejection, unhappiness and guilt, as well as producing a total or partial incapacity to enjoy the things and events of everyday life (anhedonia). Depressive disorders may be accompanied, to a greater or lesser degree, by anxiety." [4] We must reflect in order to describe that suffering and give it a name:

> "The fear of loneliness is natural and entirely understandable, but it often becomes a source of mistaken decisions, of genuinely tortuous psychological states, and of errors driven by very varied and questionable reasons.
>
> If we observe how the fear of loneliness manifests itself, we will find that it is always tied to a basic human need: our relationships with other people. If I have relationships I do not feel alone, and if I fail to have them I feel frustrated." [5]

> "A Buddhist parable comes to mind that has always struck me; it appears in the books under the name of 'The Mustard Seed'. In summary, it reflects the grief of a mother who has lost her son but who nevertheless trusts she can bring him back to life through the magical arts of the Buddha. He does not discourage the mother; he only asks that, in order to revive her son, she bring him a mustard seed obtained from a household where misfortune is unknown...
>
> The end of the parable is obvious: the mustard seed, that most particular seed, will never appear, and the mother's grief will be softened, in part, on seeing how many and how great the sufferings of all other human beings are too. But the fact that all people suffer neither removes nor explains the reality of suffering. And once again we ask ourselves: why?" (Delia Steinberg Guzmán) [8]

### The cause of suffering

#### Focusing questions

Why do I feel this way? What brought it about? What happens if we really do find ourselves at some point without the person we love, without children, without friends, without support and without understanding? Does this mean that life, for us, has ended?

#### Reflection

Just as with physical illness, depression is only the outer layer of an iceberg whose reason for being lies much deeper. It is here, after much reflection, that we must come as close as we can to the root cause of the problem. Problems in relationships are only symptoms. To quote Sikirich:

> "To answer this question one has to go beyond the surface, to concentrate on the hidden part of the iceberg that cannot immediately be seen or understood. And then it becomes clear that the problem of loneliness cannot be identified solely with the fact of having or not having relationships. Problems in relationships are the consequence, but not the cause, of loneliness.
>
> If we wish to know true love, friendship and happiness, we have to resolve fundamental problems bound up with the needs of our own Soul. And these needs are not determined by the opinion of others, nor by the way they treat us; they depend exclusively on ourselves, on our capacity to understand the deep meaning of life and the Laws of Nature, of Man and of the Universe." [5]

### The cessation of suffering

#### Focusing questions

What must I detach from in order to remove the suffering? Which ideas about myself must I adjust in order to free myself from desire?

#### Reflection

Once the suffering and its cause have been identified, it is necessary to go further and be willing to detach from the desire that leads us into this circle of suffering. The third noble truth is the assertion that distress can be transformed. This is something positive; it is the good news that transforming distress is possible. What in older language is the ceasing of distress. The ceasing of the origin of distress. There is hope, there is something certain, because some of us are capable of transforming that distress, that suffering. Some among us have overcome depression. That too is a truth you must accept.

The Buddha stressed clear sight of reality as it is, without the least distortion or interpretation. From a technical point of view, the Buddha avoided theoretical and metaphysical constructions that are difficult to measure and of debatable use for the problems of everyday life. His whole approach tends toward making a person live the here and now more fully. So Buddhist practices, and meditation in particular, lead to a clearer and more direct view of the present, reducing the time a person loses in memories of the past and in anticipation of, or craving for, the future. Meditation is therefore one of the best antidotes for depressed patients.

## Financial problems

### The existence of suffering

#### Focusing questions

What situation am I in? What is it about this situation that hurts or troubles me? What is the "bad" thing that happened to me?

#### Reflection

To analyse the suffering that exists, it is necessary to be honest and to set out the unspoken reality of our situation in order to identify the points that are "not right" financially. The problem may be that "the money does not stretch"; even so, one would have to go into more detail in order to describe the problem concretely — for example, "I do not have enough money to pay the food bills", or "the money is too little for my basic needs", and so on.

It may also be that the suffering lies in not being able to buy the things one "would like". Other financial problems that carry this kind of suffering might be unemployment, fraud, unforeseen expenses, and so on.

### The cause of suffering

#### Focusing questions

How did I arrive at this state? What decisions did I make? Which illusions did I take for realities? To what, or to whom, am I unable to say no?

#### Reflection

Financial problems, like many other problems (pains) of our personality, do not come of their own accord. We have made decisions that have brought us to this reality that hurts us. All these states, as the Buddha said, begin in the mind. And the decisions that led us to this suffering have to do with false "ideas", which fall into two categories:

- Illusions we take for reality.
- Desire for things, feelings or people that are temporary.

So, for example, the purchase of a perishable product such as an expensive meal, outside the budget but satisfying an immediate pleasure, is a desire for something destined to be used up very quickly.

Another example is the perception that in buying something on credit (with a credit card) we are making use of our own money, which is not real: it is borrowed money that we will eventually have to pay back, with interest.

A very frequent case is wanting to buy things that do not fit our way of life, because we live in a constant illusion of having more than we need for the life we ought to be leading. This leads us to spend more and more, or to model our purchases on what the media and consumer society demand that we acquire.

### The cessation of suffering

#### Focusing questions

What must I let go of? Which pleasure must I quieten? What are my priorities? Which of my responsibilities are urgent, which important, which basic and which trivial?

## Conclusions

The four noble truths, set out by the Buddha more than 2,600 years ago, are still valid in our lives today.

The cause of suffering is found in the decisions we have taken voluntarily, and these decisions originate in ideas of our own, which we must accept and change if necessary.

Suffering is an instrument of consciousness that allows us to become aware of causes, in order to balance our life in search of inner harmony.
