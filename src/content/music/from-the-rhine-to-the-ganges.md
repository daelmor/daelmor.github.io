## Introduction

This paper explores the significant influence that Indian philosophy had on the life and work of the composer Ludwig van Beethoven. Through an analysis of various sources, it takes up how Beethoven, during a critical period of his life between 1812 and 1818, immersed himself in reading Oriental and philosophical texts in search of consolation and of answers to his personal and existential dilemmas. The paper highlights the presence of references to India in Beethoven's _Tagebuch_ (diary), emphasising that these mentions were not superficial but part of a deep connection with, and admiration for, Indian wisdom.

The research goes into how the ideals of asceticism, the notion of an immaterial and omnipresent god, and the concept of dharma influenced not only Beethoven's personal outlook but also his musical work, suggesting a transition towards a more spiritual and metaphysical expression in his compositions. Some specific pieces are examined, such as the _Eroica_ Symphony and the late string quartets, bringing out how these works reflect a significant change in his spiritual and aesthetic approach, possibly inspired by his study of Indian philosophy.

The paper also considers the cultural and literary context of the period, the availability of texts about India in Europe, and how Beethoven's personal crisis and his search for a personal ethos are intrinsically related to his reflections on Hindu spirituality and ethics.

## Beethoven's interest in India

To take up Beethoven's interest in India and the evidence that supports it, we must direct our attention to a specific period of his life, between 1812 and 1818. During these years Beethoven kept a personal record known as the _Tagebuch_, or Diary. This was not, however, a diary in the conventional sense, full of meticulously dated daily entries; it was instead a compilation of reflections, many of them extracts from readings the composer was exploring at the time, interspersed with his own thoughts and comments. The record might more accurately be described as a collection of spiritual meditations than as a traditional diary.

Bernhard Appel, a leading researcher on the relationship between Beethoven and India, has pointed out a fundamental aspect: Beethoven found writing an arduous task. Therefore the act of setting something down on paper was, for him, indicative of a deep interest in, or a special connection with, the subject at hand. The notes Beethoven made in his _Tagebuch_ were not, according to Appel, mere passing jottings, but reflections intended for prolonged consideration. This approach to writing suggests that any mention or allusion to India in his notes was not trivial but a reflection of genuine interest on the composer's part — an interest that deserved to be explored and pondered carefully.

The annotations Beethoven made on the fragments gathered in his Diary were not frequent, but even so they give us an invaluable perspective on his mental process during periods of adversity. In the final years of the Napoleonic wars and afterwards, Beethoven faced significant personal challenges: he abandoned, with great sorrow, the idea of marrying; the financial support of his aristocratic patrons dwindled; his income became uncertain; and his capacity to compose notably diminished. In addition, deafness and other health problems caused him constant suffering. In this context his Diary became a record of his search for consolation and a means of defining new principles to guide his conduct and his life. In his forties Beethoven underwent an existential crisis, documented in the diary he wrote from 1812 to 1818, which led him to read classical, Oriental and contemporary sources, to explore religious and philosophical questions of ultimate meaning, and to dedicate himself again, as a martyr, to his art (Solomon, 2003).

Within this search for relief and understanding, Beethoven immersed himself in the literature of various eras and cultures, as part of his continual effort to reach the deepest wisdom of humanity. This inclination towards universal knowledge is evident in a letter he wrote to his publisher on 22 November 1809, where his choice of reading reflects an attempt to understand and apply the great teachings of history and philosophy to his own life, at a moment when he needed guidance and consolation more than ever:

> No written work could be too erudite for me, though I make no claim to the slightest personal erudition; but I have striven since childhood to grasp the meaning of the best and wisest people of every age; shame on an artist who does not believe he has the responsibility to do at least this much.

Beethoven's fascination with India formed part of his broader exploration of the world's cultures and wisdoms. In his Diary he devoted seven entries to India — more than to any other subject or place. To put this in perspective, Homer, one of his favourite authors, appears in five entries, while Herder and Schiller, also among his favourites, appear three times each. Curiously, Goethe, despite being one of the figures he most admired, does not appear in the Diary at all. Although some mentions of India might seem trivial at first sight, closer examination reveals their relevance. The references to fundamental texts of Indian literature and thought, in particular, deserve special analysis.

Beethoven's interest in India arouses curiosity for several reasons, challenging the notion that his genius was exclusively a product of European civilisation. This leads us to ask how deep his interest in India was, what drew him to it, and what his sources of information were. The scarcity of evidence raises the challenge of how to maximise its interpretive value, and which approaches might offer credible conclusions about Beethoven's relationship with India. Scholars ask whether his interest transcended European art and thought, or whether "India" was no more than a Romantic fantasy.

The path to this understanding is complex and many-sided. It begins with the Diary entries themselves, which contain revealing extracts and clues to his reaction to these texts. These clues are not sufficient on their own, however; they must be complemented by an analysis of the sources of the extracts, the German literary context of the period, the European intermediaries who gave Beethoven access to these works, the historical moment of Europe between the French empire and the post-Napoleonic order, and the situation of India during the shift of power between the Mughal Empire and the British Raj.

The most convincing interpretation of Beethoven's annotations is arrived at by considering two principal factors: the social anxieties that motivated the interest of educated Germans in India, and Beethoven's personal crisis. Germans of the period accessed knowledge about India mainly through British sources, but interpreted it in the light of their own social and political experience. As for Beethoven, the Indian texts served him as a spiritual guide, offering ascetic and heroic counsel for navigating the moral dilemmas he faced during his years of crisis.

## Deciphering Beethoven's resonance with India

Beethoven's Diary, known as the _Tagebuch_, is famous not only among those who study his life and music; it was also accessible to those who wrote about him first. This diary has been key to understanding Beethoven more deeply. Maynard Solomon was the first to publish it formally, adding commentary that aids comprehension. Sieghard Brandenburg then took up the baton and offered a version still more complete than Solomon's, including images of the original diary. Thanks to the work of Solomon and Brandenburg we can feel closer to the age in which Beethoven lived, through the composer's own direct words and the detailed analysis of these experts.

Within the scholarly literature on the Diary, musicologists have in recent years paid particular attention to Beethoven's references to India. The first important contribution comes, again, from Maynard Solomon, who wrote about Beethoven and India in two articles on Beethoven and Freemasonry. Beethoven's interest in India figures in these essays as part of his broader orientation "towards the East", as Solomon puts it.

Beyond Solomon's isolated remarks, Bernhard Appel has written specifically on Beethoven and India. He made the spectacular discovery that all of Beethoven's references go back to a single study of India's past and present. This source appeared in a larger series: Eberhard August Wilhelm Zimmermann's _Taschenbuch der Reisen, oder unterhaltende Darstellung der Entdeckungen des 18. Jahrhunderts, in Rücksicht der Länder-, Menschen- und Productenkunde, für jede Klasse von Lesern_ (Pocket book of travels, or entertaining account of the discoveries of the eighteenth century, with regard to the study of countries, peoples and products, for every class of reader). Almost all of Beethoven's extracts come from the second volume on India, dated 1813.

## Personal struggle and the search for dharma

The seven Diary entries dealing with India date from late 1815 and 1816 (Solomon, 1977). To interpret them, we can begin with an overview of their subjects. Three describe Brahmin ascetic practices — for example, a long period of silence for novices entering the priesthood. Beethoven also includes a description of the Creation; two paragraphs describing respectively the majesty and the immateriality of God; one on spiritual purification; one on a Catholic priest's reaction to a lingam, or phallic stone sculpture; and another on polyandry in present-day Kerala. Here is a fragment of one of these entries:

> "God is immaterial; since He is invisible, He has no form. But from what we can see in His works, we deduce that He is eternal, almighty, omniscient and omnipresent. Only the mighty one is free of all desire and passion. There is no one and nothing greater than He, Brahma: His mind is self-existent. He, the Almighty, is present in every part of space. Omniscience is the greatest of all His attributes, which encompass everything. O God! Thou hast no threefold being and art independent of all things; Thou art the true, eternal, blessed and unchanging light of all time and space. Thy wisdom apprehends thousands of laws, yet Thou always actest by Thine own will, free, and for Thine own honour. Thou art prior to everything we worship. To Thee we owe praise and adoration. Thou alone art the truly Blessed, the best of all laws, the image of all wisdom. Thou art present throughout the whole world and sustainest all things. Sun, Ether, Brahma."

On music specifically, Zimmermann spoke of Indian music and dance, but Beethoven only briefly noted the names of the notes of the Indian scale.

Taken together, Beethoven's entries fall into three groups: religious passages relating Brahmin ideas about the supreme deity and human aspirations towards the divine; the ethics of human action in the world; and ethnographic material. It is the second, ethical, category that relates most directly to Beethoven's search for his personal ethos — though this search is bound up with his religious beliefs and finds some expression in his choice of ethnography.

Beethoven's extracts on India begin with Zimmermann's discussion of the religion of the Hindus. His first two entries copy texts from Zimmermann praising the Brahmin conceptions of a universal creator God and of a disinterested morality, superior in sublimity, according to Zimmermann, to Greek, Hebrew or other Asian conceptions of the Supreme Being. Beethoven extracts passages that emphasise God's unlimited dominion through time and space and man's upward striving to be united with his Creator in a state of perfect purity:

> Spirit of spirits
>
> spread through all space and time
>
> rising above the limits of thought
>
> Thou hast created order out of chaos.
>
> Before the world was, Thou wast.
>
> Before the heavens above and the earth below
>
> Thou didst exist alone.
>
> Through love Thou hast created all who worship Thee.
>
> Why dost Thou not manifest Thy power and goodness without limit?
>
> What bright light directed Thy power?
>
> How was Thine infinite wisdom first set forth?
>
> Direct my mind and raise it from the depths.

His third entry on India, also concerned with religion, calls for our special attention, since it contains a call to action. It gathers three different passages that Zimmermann chose from two different sources. There is a paradoxical change of mood from the first passage to the second and third. The first, written in elevated language, envisages a dynamic movement from earthly corruption towards a state of greater purity:

> "Everything flowed pure and clear from God. If passion should darken me once more with evil, then through atonement and purification I would return many times to the first pure source, to the Divinity — and — to thy Art."

Phrases full of longing and striving. The purity of God is his point of departure; this purity is not passive but flows towards an existence stained by human passion, which amounts to evil. After the darkness comes the dynamic movement again, the return to the divine. The last phrase — "and — to thy art" — is Beethoven's own addition.

With it, the religious and the aesthetic overlap. Beethoven neither denies the struggle towards God nor translates it into an aesthetic mode, as the modernist artists of the late nineteenth century would; instead he intensifies the meaning of his art by interweaving it with a religious search (Liebersohn, 2023).

This double search, religious and aesthetic, unfolds in solitude. Yet the solitude of the artist, set apart from society to follow his vocation with almost revolutionary intensity, is not an end point in the Diary. Rather, Beethoven alternates between the longing for complete withdrawal — at one moment, in a terse note appearing shortly after the first run of entries on India, he plays with the idea of retiring to a peasant farm — and guilt and sadness at the gulf between himself and his fellow men, followed by a determination to serve them.

Immediately after the description of religious and artistic striving as a solitary struggle, the second and third parts of the entry turn towards passages that move in the direction of social responsibility. They come from a work that dramatises the tensions between personal and public obligations: the famous Sanskrit masterpiece of the dramatist Kalidasa, _The Recognition of Shakuntala_. Dating possibly from the late fourth or fifth century AD, possibly written at the court of the Gupta dynasty, the play dazzles and enchants in evoking a magically living nature complemented by the political sphere; adorably recognisable characters, from the humblest peasant to the warrior king; soliloquy written with great eloquence, conjoining dynastic ambition and cosmic destiny.

Kalidasa's play takes dramatic shape after King Dushyanta meets Shakuntala in a sacred forest where she is an ascetic and the adoptive daughter of the great ascetic Kanva. Dushyanta and Shakuntala are overwhelmed by the passion they feel for each other, but not alarmed, since ancient Hindu custom recognised a _gandharva_ form of marriage for royalty in which the sexual act could be followed by a legal public union, provided the woman gave her consent. King Dushyanta is called back to his duties at court; the lovers agree that Shakuntala will join him at a later date. But when she visits him at his palace, by then visibly pregnant, an evil spell has smothered the memory of their encounter. The passage Beethoven copied comes from the moment when Shakuntala and her retinue arrive and the king turns to acknowledge the presence of the visitors. One of the king's contented priests praises his master for this noble gesture. But the priest accompanying Shakuntala refuses to see any special merit in it: it is the king's duty to share his riches, including the acknowledgement of his visitors. He must put his dharma into practice, the cosmic destiny belonging to his rank. This theme of duty imposed from above persists throughout the scene. The ascetics and court counsellors admonish the king to fulfil his duties; in this case, at their suggestion, to acknowledge and marry Shakuntala. The play's message rejects isolating asceticism. Instead of renunciation it preaches a balance between the demands of the individual and of society, and offers the prospect of their happy resolution (Liebersohn, 2023).

Beethoven gave a personal meaning to the obligation of duty towards others, in particular towards the great of the earth, the "benefactors of humanity", changing the wording of Zimmermann's text so that it applied to himself. In Zimmermann (and behind him, Forster's translation), the ascetic leading Shakuntala's party says: "That was our wish; no selfish interest animates us." Beethoven changes it to read: "Let no selfish interest animate _you_" — that is, Beethoven himself. The composer, no less than a king, was a benefactor of humanity with an obligation to serve it.

Hence we have two successive passages in the Diary in which Beethoven signals his meaning by altering them. The first sketches a solitary call to self-purification; the second commands the great to serve those beneath them. Together they sum up Beethoven's twin beliefs in the need to turn both inward and outward — purifying himself through dedication to his art, and sharing his inner riches with humanity.

> "Blessed is the man, it is said, who has suppressed all the passions and then with all his strength performs all the business of life, without concerning himself with success."

Inner detachment is the key: personal passions disappear and one acts with far greater force. The phrase also contains another important teaching in ending with contempt for worldly success. One undertakes actions because they are right in themselves; autonomous action leads to the fulfilment of duty. Inner detachment and dedication to his art were what Beethoven too was seeking; this passage also fits with his insistence on action and its efficacy in resolving moral dilemmas:

> "Let not thy life pass in inaction. Fixed in Yoga, perform thy duty, abandon all thought of consequences and make action equal; abandon attachment, with a mind serene in success and in failure; for such uniformity of mind is called Yoga, attention to the spiritual."

This piece of wisdom from the _Bhagavad Gita_ does not merely show but tells the listener what to do, demanding a discipline that forms the inner being. But this concern with oneself was not to be a private act of self-improvement. Zimmermann makes clear throughout his text that the discipline is in the service of a wider, socially formed order. Krishna's ethic of duty was different from that of _Shakuntala_. His dialogue recommends disinterested action within the world, but leads beyond it towards spiritual ends; whereas the play's conclusion refers back from a celestial scene to the world with the founding of a royal dynasty. Each of these works, in its own way, spoke of the tensions between self and altruism, between suffering and duty towards one's vocation, which Beethoven experienced personally and reflected on in his Diary.

Ascetic devotion to art and the satisfaction of a longing for social engagement were not mutually exclusive. He withdrew thereafter into a circle of connoisseurs able to follow the rarefied spiritual adventure of his late string quartets and piano sonatas. Even while making an inner retreat and struggling to detach himself from the world, Beethoven remained loyal to the revolutionary ideal of universal brotherhood. As early as the late 1790s he had begun sketching music for Schiller's _Ode to Joy_, the highest expression of that ideal; he returned to it again in 1805 and 1812 before finally placing it in the triumphant conclusion of the Ninth Symphony, premiered in 1824.

In sum, for Beethoven music was the channelling of the divine towards humanity:

> "When I open my eyes I sigh, for all I see is contrary to my worship, and I am forced to despise this world, incapable of understanding that music is a revelation higher than all wisdom and all philosophy. Yes, like a generous wine, music gives inspiration, and I am the Bacchus who harvests the wine on which humanity grows drunk. Once sober, there is in it nothing but an indigestible mixture of confused ideas. I have no friends, my life must pass in solitude, but I know that God is closer to me in my art than to others. I walk with Him without fear, for I have always recognised and understood Him. As for my music, I feel no anxiety about it; nothing bad can happen to it. Whoever has once understood it must feel freed from all the miseries in which others struggle."

## Influence on his music

It is possible that the function of the music of Beethoven's deepest and most reflective period is to communicate valuable spiritual states, and that these states bear witness to the depth of the artist's nature and the quality of his experience of life. Generally these states cannot be correlated with specific situations, and for this reason no concrete interpretation can be given for them. They are the fruits of innumerable experiences of the artist, and they enter into the very texture of his spiritual being.

Beethoven, like those artists who master the Power of Will over matter, stands before silence — the beginning, where the pure Idea, freed from the prison of matter, is to fertilise the world of indefinite form in order to "give birth" to the work of art, faithful creature of Divine Inspiration. Thus there come together in him a series of characteristics answering to those two fundamental aspects of art: the formal element and the inner element (Padilla, 2006).

With pantheism inspiring his soul, and with his natural metaphysical elevation and love of knowledge, it is logical that reading the Upanishads should have left him marvelling. This communication, lived through music as an expressive art, ought to evoke states of consciousness in the listener. In the words of Teresa Berganza:

> "The artist, the true artist, bears upon himself the weight of a priesthood which, with inescapable demands, enslaves him to the work of serving as a bridge between the god that is Beauty and men, who are always hungry for divinity."

Beethoven's attitude to life was conditioned to a great extent by certain fundamental experiences. It is quite possible that the development and transformation of Beethoven's attitude to life, the result of certain fundamental experiences, can be traced in his music (Sullivan, 1927).

Below are mentioned some pieces written by Beethoven during the period of his spiritual search through Indian philosophy. This period genuinely marks a significant change from his earlier periods in the spiritual approach of his music.

The first musical piece Beethoven composed with a really deep and important spiritual content is the _Eroica_ Symphony. Indeed, the difference from the earlier music is so surprising that it points to an almost catastrophic change, or an extremely rapid acceleration in his spiritual development. The Heiligenstadt Testament bears witness to this change, and we shall see that the _Eroica_ is an astonishingly realised and coordinated expression of the spiritual experiences underlying that document.

There are scholars who say that the Upanishads had a great influence on the conception of his _Missa solemnis_. Sullivan remarks that "only a very great spirit could have written the _Missa Solemnis_."

The mathematics and music of the world of ideas, with teachings as vital and abstract as those of these Vedic texts, is very easy to compare with that of the late string quartets, since it is in this period that Beethoven would have meditated most on this religion-mathematics-philosophy-music that the various Upanishads are — and where, beyond a personal God, a mere focus radiating divine light, one breathes eternity, law, Perfect Order (dharma), and man's responsibility and struggle against destiny and death (karma) (Fernández, 2021).

Precisely a few years after the last Diary entry in 1818, Beethoven would write these string quartets, which are rightly considered his finest musical compositions and very probably among the most sublime works in the whole repertoire of Western classical music. In their day these late quartets (numbers 12–16 and the _Grosse Fuge_) were regarded with suspicion, as works almost incomprehensible and confused. Beethoven's contemporary, the composer Franz Schubert, was exceptional in recognising that the late string quartets were perhaps an expression of the ineffable in human existence and of the soul's search for the transcendent. On hearing the String Quartet No. 14 in C-sharp minor (Op. 131) for the last time, just before his own death, a year after Beethoven's passing, Schubert exclaimed: "After this, what is left for us to write?"

The poet and philosopher T. S. Eliot may have taken inspiration from these quartets when he later wrote, in poetry and associated with the four elements (earth, water, air and fire), his _Four Quartets_ — the poem for which he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1948. It is thought to be in relation to No. 15, Op. 132, that he wrote, in a letter:

> "Its study is absolutely inexhaustible. There is a sort of heavenly, or at least more than human, gaiety about some of his later things which one imagines to be the fruit of reconciliation and relief after immense suffering. I should like to get something of that into verse before I die." (Fernández, 2020)

The String Quartet No. 16, Op. 135, is Beethoven's last important work. Its title is _The Difficult Decision_, from the annotation at the start of the final movement, with the first chords — low, slow and sombre — asking: "Must it be?", to which the main theme, swift and cheerful, answers imperatively: "It must be!" It represents the final acceptance of destiny, of death; the hero gives himself over to it, and the work has concluded.

It is surprising but necessary to mention that certain musicologists have been inclined to consider how Indian philosophy may have contributed to creating, within the frame of Beethoven's thought, a space for the melancholy longing for liberation that Buddhists describe as Nirvana and Hindus as Moksha. "After the Upanishads, India must recognise that Beethoven has given us the music of _advaita_" (Lal, 2021).

Beethoven's capacity to understand the fundamental character of life in its two aspects of pain and pleasure, combined with his lack of flexibility, was the necessary condition for the development of his attitude to life. That development takes the form of a synthesis. The Beethoven of the C minor symphony finds the meaning of life in achievement in spite of pain. Destiny is an enemy to be defied. The Beethoven of the late quartets finds that the highest achievement is reached through suffering. This pain is accepted as a necessary condition of life, as an illuminating power. His music makes it evident that the reconciliation was genuine and complete, and these examples of Beethoven's pieces are the expression of an authentic experience. The quality of this experience has led many writers to call this music "mystical" or "metaphysical".

As Michael Parloff says in his lecture cycle on the late quartets, this last movement, the fourth, of his final composition is preceded, in the third, by a kind of prayer that makes the music undulate, generating a sense of infinity. From there the fatal question will arise five times, and after answering cheerfully that yes, "it must be", the music will finally sink into silence (Fernández, 2020).

## Conclusions

This monograph has offered a deep reflection on how Ludwig van Beethoven's immersion in Indian philosophy not only enriched his personal life in times of crisis, but also infused a new dimension of spirituality and depth into his musical work. Through this study it is revealed that Beethoven's interest in India transcends mere curiosity, becoming a vital influence that permeated both his thought and his artistic creation.

The study of his _Tagebuch_, together with the exploration of his musical compositions during this period, illustrates how the concepts of dharma, detachment and the search for union with the divine are reflected in his music, offering a deeper and more universal perspective on the human condition. Beethoven's work, marked by these influences, is not only a testament to his musical genius but also to his tireless search for meaning and consolation in the wisdom of distant cultures.

Beethoven's fascination with and respect for India constitute an emblematic example of how art can serve as a bridge between different worlds, cultures and ages, demonstrating the universality of the human desire to understand the essence of our existence in its highest form. It is a manifestation of the shared human search for transcendence and spiritual connection.
